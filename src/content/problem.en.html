Where this began

This project began with a personal observation: my personal details did not match on two government websites. This reflects the way Kazakhstan's digital government is organised.

By international measures, Kazakhstan is a leader in digital government. In 2026 it rose from 24th to 16th place in the UN E-Government Development Index (EGDI), with a score of 0.9363 (UN DESA 2026). Around 90% of public services are available online, and eGov.kz has over 15.1 million registered users [gov.kz].

However, eGov does not produce the data it displays. According to the head of NIT, which operates eGov, the information comes from separate state registries, such as civil, registration and medical records. If a citizen finds someone else's child or spouse in their account, the error lies in the source registry, and only the agency responsible for it can correct it [Digital Business]. The portal displays data from many databases that do not always agree.

Who is affected and at what scale

In principle, almost all of the country's roughly 20 million residents have state records. The scale of the problem is clearest in data security.

In June 2025, a file containing 16.3 million records, with names, individual identification numbers (IIN), addresses and phone numbers, became publicly accessible. The Prime Minister stated that preliminary findings pointed to medical organisations as the source [Tengrinews]. The Interior Ministry reported more than 40 major data breaches since the start of 2025 and confirmed that access to citizens' personal data is being sold illegally [Tengrinews].

Insiders also play a role: a Public Service Centre (ЦОН) employee in Astana was convicted of deliberately entering false information into a state system in October 2024, based on documents submitted by an unknown person [Kazpravda].

How the problem is changing

The risk has been growing. Each incident involved a different organisation holding data on the same people.

At the same time, the state has begun to show citizens how their data is used. Since July 2026, eGov accounts display which state bodies have requested a person's data [Lada.kz]. A consent service (via SMS from 1414) allows people to approve or refuse access by companies [gov.kz].

However, this information is hard to interpret.

{{icon:alert}}

Access “until 2092”

In February 2026, one user shared what eGov showed her about access to her data by companies: a company (SK Amanat) with access to her data “until 2092”, and a bank that had viewed her data 13 times in six months. The feature did not show when access had been granted or which data had been used [Finratings.kz].

This is one user's report, which the outlet did not verify, so it is used only as an illustration.

In an eGov Mobile survey of more than 50,000 users, about 53% said they did not know whom to contact if their data was compromised, and only 18% said they knew what to do [egov.kz].

The social-science question

The problem is mainly institutional rather than technical. Administrative burden theory (Moynihan, Herd & Harvey 2015) describes the learning, psychological and compliance costs that citizens bear in their dealings with the state. In Kazakhstan's digital government, citizens bear the cost of the state's own data errors: they must notice the error, identify the responsible agency and prove that the record is wrong.

The new access logs add a second problem, which Fox (2007) describes as “opaque” transparency: information is released, but it does not reveal how institutions behave or make them answerable. This project therefore asks:

Who bears the cost of the state's data errors, and can a citizen-facing AI service shift this burden back to the institutions that create the errors, without becoming another tool of surveillance?
