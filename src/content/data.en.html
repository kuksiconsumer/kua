What is currently measured

  • UN DESA measures online government services. Kazakhstan's EGDI rose from 0.387 in 2003 to 0.901 in 2024 and 0.936 in 2026 [UN DESA].
  • The Bureau of National Statistics publishes annual household ICT statistics: the share of internet users aged 6–74, digital literacy, purposes of internet use, and broadband coverage.
  • The Ministry of AI and Digital Development publishes user numbers and some enforcement data. It carried out 77 personal-data inspections in 2025 and a further 44 in 2026 at the time of reporting [Kapital.kz]. In the first seven months of 2026, the authorities registered 90 criminal offences related to personal-data protection (compared with 172 a year earlier), and 58 people were convicted [Zakon.kz].
  • The largest dataset on citizens' experience of service problems is a national eGov survey of 27,336 users conducted in September–October 2024 and published by Amirova (2025). Each respondent identified the main problem they had faced.

None of these sources measures whether the records themselves are accurate.

About two in five users named the state's own systems and data as their main obstacle. This is probably an underestimate of data errors, because a citizen usually sees only that “the system failed”, not that one registry contradicts another.

The reporting gap

In 2025, the ministry received only 5 official notifications of personal-data leaks, and 2 in 2026, all from private organisations. By September 2025, the Interior Ministry had counted more than 40 major breaches. This suggests that the regulator learns about only a small share of incidents.

A high score is not the same as accountability

Kazakhstan's EGDI has risen almost every survey since 2003. In the same year that it reached 16th place, Freedom House rated Kazakhstan “Not Free” (23/100). A high score for online services is not in itself evidence of accountable government (Linde & Karlsson 2013).

{{fig3}}

Gaps in the data

  1. No public figure exists on how often registries contradict each other. Neither the ministry, nor NIT, nor the Bureau publishes a mismatch rate. The central issue of this project is therefore unmeasured, which is itself a finding.
  2. Rural citizens. 98.4% of Amirova's respondents lived in cities, and the survey was conducted on egov.kz itself, so people with limited internet access were not included.
  3. People with disabilities. The ITU found that data on broadband access, internet use and digital literacy among people with disabilities is lacking [ITU].
  4. Data leaks. Reporting depends on organisations notifying the regulator, and most do not (Figure 2).
  5. Costs. In November 2026, the ministry told BES.media that it could not provide a total cost of AI deployment, and it declined to share its registry of AI projects [BES.media].

How Kuä would help fill these gaps

Kuä would record every mismatch it detects, anonymised and by registry. Its public scorecard would produce the first national data on how often registries disagree, which agencies are responsible for most errors and how long corrections take, broken down by region and language. This would give the government, the Supreme Audit Chamber and Parliament an indicator they could act on.
